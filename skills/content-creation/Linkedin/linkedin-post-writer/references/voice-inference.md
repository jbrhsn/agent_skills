# Voice Inference

The post must sound like the person who wrote `source.md`. Read this at workflow step 2.

## Why this matters

There is a default LinkedIn voice — punchy, motivational, slightly breathless, heavy on one-line paragraphs and manufactured profundity. It is instantly recognizable and increasingly distrusted. Defaulting to it makes every author sound like every other author, which is precisely the opposite of what topical authority requires.

`source.md` is unpolished, which makes it the most honest available sample of how the author actually thinks and writes. Mine it rather than overwriting it. **The transformation is structural, not tonal.** Reorganize the material; don't relocate the voice.

## Signals to extract

Read `source.md` specifically for these before drafting anything.

**Formality.** Contractions present or absent? Slang, profanity, casual asides? An author writing "yeah this broke immediately" and an author writing "the deployment failed on initialization" need different posts.

**Technical density.** Are domain terms used bare, or explained on first use? This reveals the assumed audience. Preserve that level — flattening jargon for a general audience insults the actual readership, and inflating it for a general one loses them.

**Sentence rhythm.** Long and clause-heavy, or clipped and declarative? This is the most transferable signal and the easiest to lose in editing.

**Stance.** Confident and declarative, or hedged and exploratory? An author who writes "I think this might be why" should not be published saying "This is exactly why."

**Humor.** Present at all? Dry, self-deprecating, sarcastic? If absent from the source, do not add any.

**First-person posture.** "I built" vs "we built" vs impersonal description. Match it. Switching a solo project to "we" or a team effort to "I" is a credibility problem, not a style choice.

**Idiosyncrasies.** Recurring phrases, unusual word choices, a specific way of framing problems. These are the highest-value things to preserve. When choosing between the author's odd word and a smoother synonym, keep the odd word.

## What to change

Structure, order, and compression are fair game — that's the actual work:

- Reorder for hook-first delivery.
- Cut tangents and everything not serving the single idea.
- Tighten rambling sentences **without changing their register.**
- Fix genuine errors.
- Add connective tissue between blocks where the source jumped.

## What to preserve

- Vocabulary level and specific word choices.
- Sentence rhythm and typical length.
- Confidence level and hedging.
- Humor, or its absence.
- Concrete details: tool names, numbers, timestamps, error strings.
- Any strong opinion — softening it into balance is the most common way a good post is ruined.

## Handling thin sources

Some `source.md` files are three bullets with no voice signal at all. In that case:

- Default to **plain, direct, technically literate prose** — the register of a competent practitioner explaining something to a peer. Not motivational, not corporate.
- Do not invent personality, backstory, or anecdote to fill the gap.
- Say plainly in the response that the source was thin on voice signal, and offer to revise if the register is wrong.

## Calibration checks

Before writing the file:

1. **Substitution test.** Could this post have been written by any of a hundred other people in this field? If yes, the voice got sanded off.
2. **Reverse test.** If the author read this post cold, would they recognize it as theirs?
3. **Escalation check.** Did the draft make any claim more confident than `source.md` made it? Walk it back.
4. **Vocabulary check.** Does the post contain words the author never uses? Replace them with words from the source.
