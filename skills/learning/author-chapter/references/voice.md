# Voice

The target is a good teacher writing for an intelligent 28-year-old who knows nothing about this particular field: warm, direct, efficient, and never condescending. Think of the best explanation you ever got from someone who genuinely wanted you to understand — not a manual, not a lecture transcript, not a blog post trying to sound clever.

Two halves, and both matter. **Knows nothing about the field:** no term goes undefined, no step is skipped as obvious, nothing is assumed from prior study. **Intelligent adult:** they have judgement, a job, and things to compare this to. Do not explain how the world works, do not reassure them, and do not pace them. Their scarce resource is time, not capacity.

## Sentence level

- Short sentences carry hard ideas. When the content gets dense, the prose gets simpler, not more formal.
- Second person, present tense. "You send a request, the server checks the cache" beats "a request is sent and the cache is consulted."
- Concrete before abstract, always. Name a real thing, then generalise from it.
- Prefer the plain word. "Use" not "utilise", "so" not "hence", "let" not "facilitate". Technical terms are fine — they're precise. Formal register is not the same as precision.
- Numbers and specifics beat adjectives. "Roughly 200 milliseconds" teaches; "quite slow" doesn't.

## What to avoid

- **Undefined jargon.** The single most common failure. If a term hasn't been defined in this document, define it right there.
- **Filler openers.** Sections start with content, not with "In this section, we will explore the fascinating world of..."
- **Hollow enthusiasm.** "This is really powerful!" tells the reader nothing. Show the power with an example and they'll supply the enthusiasm.
- **Rhetorical questions used as headings** ("But what is a hash table?"). Ask questions only when the reader is meant to actually attempt an answer.
- **Bullet-point explanation.** Bullets are for lists of parallel things. Reasoning needs prose — a bulleted chain of logic loses the connective tissue that makes it reasoning.
- **Bold-everything emphasis.** If four phrases per paragraph are bold, none of them are emphasised.
- **The passive voice hiding an actor.** "It is recommended that" — by whom, and why? Name the actor or drop the claim.
- **Summarising what you're about to say, then saying it, then summarising it again.** Say it once, well.
- **Talking down.** No "don't worry, this is easy!" — if it were easy the reader would already know it. And never call something simple that took the reader a page to get through; that just teaches them they're slow.

## Handling difficulty

When something is genuinely hard, say so and stay with it: "This is the part that takes people two or three passes. Here it is slowly." That's respect, and it keeps the reader from concluding they're the problem.

When something is arbitrary, say so: "There's no deep reason for this name — it's historical." Learners burn real time hunting for logic that isn't there.

## Length

**Bias complete per unit, then end the unit.** Write until the idea is genuinely taught, then stop — do not keep going because the topic is interesting, and do not compress a real trade-off because the file is already long.

The two failures are not symmetrical, and neither is fixed by a word count. A unit that stops short leaves the reader unable to do the thing. A unit that runs on leaves them unable to find where it ended. Completeness against the brief decides what goes in; the five-minute target decides only how it is cut up.

If a unit will not fit in roughly five minutes without losing something real, take the extra minutes. If it fits in three, do not stretch it. If it needs fifteen, it is two units.
