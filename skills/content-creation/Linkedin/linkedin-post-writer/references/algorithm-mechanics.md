# Distribution evidence and editorial decisions

Platform systems evolve, and published engineering details do not reveal a reliable formula for an individual post. Distinguish platform statements, account analytics, observational studies, and editorial hypotheses.

LinkedIn describes relevance and multiple engagement predictions in its [feed engineering overview](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/feed/engineering-the-next-generation-of-linkedins-feed) and discusses [dwell-time modeling](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/feed/leveraging-dwell-time-to-improve-member-experiences-on-the-linkedin-feed). These sources do not establish a universal link penalty, ideal post length, comment-to-like multiplier, hashtag quota, or first-hour cutoff.

## Practical use

Make the intended audience and topic clear, give readers substantive value, and fulfill the opening's promise. Useful citations can belong in the body; a first-comment link is an editorial option, not a guaranteed distribution advantage. Do not sacrifice attribution to a speculative ranking tactic.

Choose timing from audience geography and the author's availability, or from their actual analytics. Without that evidence, label a proposed time as an experiment. No universal weekday or time guarantees reach.

Use tags, emojis, and questions only when they serve meaning and fit the author. Avoid manufactured urgency, deceptive hooks, reciprocal engagement schemes, and empty requests for reactions.

## Evaluate after publishing

Match metrics to purpose: qualified responses, relevant visits, leads, understanding, or sustained readership may matter more than impressions. Compare similar formats, audiences, and observation windows. Distinguish correlation from cause; changing a title, time, and format together cannot isolate any one effect.

Report a source and date for quantitative platform claims. If supporting evidence is unavailable, remove the number rather than repeat creator folklore as a hard rule.
