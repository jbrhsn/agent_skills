# Structure options

Choose an order that follows the learner's questions and dependencies. The outline below is an organizational option, not default headings to reproduce. Prefer topic-specific headings and connected prose for a complete chapter; see [chapter-example.md](chapter-example.md) for a finished illustration. A short explanation, problem set, advanced reference, or discovery exercise may need a different shape.

```markdown
# <Chapter>

Opening takeaway or motivating problem.

## Core concepts
### <A manageable unit>
### <Another unit>

## Deeper applications
## Putting it together
## Practice or recall
## Sources and further reading
```

A contents list helps long documents. A glossary helps vocabulary-heavy ones. A core/deeper split helps when some material can be postponed, but there is no fixed core percentage. Reading-time estimates are optional and approximate.

## Units

A unit should leave the reader with a usable idea. Roughly five minutes is one useful target, not a cap or minimum. Choose boundaries around understanding rather than word counts. Recap prerequisite ideas enough for someone returning after a break without repeatedly reteaching them.

Consider these prompts where useful:

- Why does this matter, and what problem does it solve?
- What is the idea, in terms this reader understands?
- What example makes the mechanism visible?
- What misconception or boundary case could mislead the reader?
- What alternatives or trade-offs affect a real decision?
- What question or task would show understanding?

These are teaching prompts, not six mandatory fields. Combine them, use them as headings, or omit those that add no value. A definition or a worked proof need not invent a trade-off to qualify as a unit.

## Progression and scaffolds

Use existing tier names consistently when they connect to a plan or tracker, and cover the levels requested by the assignment. A tier list is context, not a demand to teach every topic to the highest level. Standalone material may use domain-appropriate stages or no named ladder.

For scaffolded material, preserve useful briefs, metadata, and navigation while replacing placeholders with teaching content. Adapt the stub's tier headings when another organization reads better. If scope changes are authorized, update the relevant brief and plan together rather than maintaining contradictory instructions.

## Examples, visuals, and code

Choose prose, tables, diagrams, code, or interactive material according to what the reader needs to understand. Explain enough context to interpret a visual. Tables can compare options and summarize short reasoning; use prose when an argument needs more room.

Code can be a complete runnable example, an excerpt, or pseudocode; identify which it is. Supply setup and expected behavior where needed, and distinguish expected output from output actually observed. Do not imply that code was executed unless it was.
