# Voice and authorship

Use the author's prose, explicit preferences, and audience as evidence for tone. Notes may include copied sources or dictation; do not mistake either for a complete style guide. Never assume professional background, personality, or credentials from a topic alone.

Notice formality, vocabulary, technical density, rhythm, humor, uncertainty, and I/we attribution. Preserve distinctive language when it remains accurate and clear. Editing may fix errors and improve readability without copying every quirk or retaining a quota of verbatim lines.

A short voice note can help a substantial rewrite: register, stance, audience knowledge, and expressions worth keeping. It need not become a mandatory artifact. Sparse notes support a plain default, not invented personality.

Check that the draft does not turn speculation into certainty, a team result into a solo achievement, a cited story into lived experience, or a criticism into a claim stronger than the evidence. State consequential changes in the accompanying notes. Ask before reversing the author's position or dropping explicitly required material; routine compression can proceed.
