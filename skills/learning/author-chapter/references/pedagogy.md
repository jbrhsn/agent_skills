# Teaching techniques

Choose techniques according to the learner, topic, and task. These are useful options, not a claim that one method is best in every setting.

## Orientation and motivation

An early takeaway helps readers organize an explanation. A motivating problem can make a mechanism meaningful. For guided discovery or assessment, allowing the reader to try before revealing the conclusion may be more appropriate.

For long material, distinguish essential concepts from optional extensions. Let dependencies and the requested outcome determine that distinction rather than a fixed 20/80 ratio.

## Cognitive load and pacing

Introduce unfamiliar ideas in manageable groups. A running example can reduce repeated setup; varied examples help transfer when the learner needs to recognize the idea in new settings. Brief prerequisite reminders help readers resume after a break.

Simplify when that makes an idea accessible, while signalling the limits that matter. Not every beginner explanation needs the full advanced correction immediately; link or defer it honestly if it lies outside scope.

## Worked examples and practice

For beginners, a worked example often provides the support needed to attempt a similar task. Gradually reduce that support as the learner gains competence. Experienced learners may benefit from starting with a challenge, diagnosis, or comparison.

Recall, prediction, transfer, and diagnosis questions serve different purposes. Choose what tests the intended capability. Place answers where the learner can attempt the question first when self-testing matters, and provide explanations or solutions when requested.

## Analogies and misconceptions

Use an analogy when it clarifies a structural relationship. Explain a boundary where extending it would mislead. A second representation can help if its relationship to the first is clear; an analogy is not required for every concept.

Address likely misconceptions with a counterexample or contrasting case. Do not invent a trap for every section simply to satisfy a template.

## Advanced judgment

Deeper material can examine alternatives, costs, boundary conditions, uncertainty, historical constraints, or decisions under real conditions. Choose the dimensions meaningful in the domain: scale for infrastructure, audience for writing, context for habits, and question strategy for an exam. Greater expertise does not require contradicting consensus or finding an undocumented fact.
