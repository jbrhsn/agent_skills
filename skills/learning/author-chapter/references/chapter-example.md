# More lemonade, same mix: understanding ratios

Imagine a drink made with one cup of lemon syrup and four cups of water. You want a larger batch with the same proportions. Adding more water alone would dilute it; adding more syrup alone would make it more concentrated. The amount can change, but the relationship between the two ingredients needs to stay the same.

That relationship is a **ratio**. Once you understand it, you can resize a recipe even when someone gives you only the final amount they want.

## What “one to four” actually tells you

The ratio of syrup to water is written **1:4**, read “one to four.” It means one measure of syrup for every four equal measures of water. The order matters: water to syrup would be 4:1. Labeling the ingredients prevents accidentally reversing the recipe.

Think of five identical measuring cups lined up: one holds syrup, and four hold water. The cups represent five equal **parts**. A part is a share of the recipe, not a fixed unit like a litre. You can choose a spoon, a cup, or another measure as one part, provided every part uses the same measure.

The row of cups is a picture of the quantities you measure before mixing; the ingredients will not remain in separate compartments afterward. For the recipe calculations here, we treat the measured liquid volumes as adding together. Recipes needing exact final volumes would require checking that assumption.

One small trap is hidden in the notation. A 1:4 syrup-to-water ratio does **not** mean syrup is one quarter of the whole drink. There are five parts altogether. Syrup is one of those five, written **1/5**, or “one fifth.” In this fraction, the bottom number counts the equal parts in the whole, and the top number counts the parts being selected. Water makes up the other four fifths, written 4/5.

## Making a larger batch without changing the relationship

Suppose you have 150 millilitres of syrup and want to use it all. A millilitre, abbreviated mL, is a unit of volume. Here, one syrup part is 150 mL, so each water part must also be 150 mL.

Four water parts therefore require 4 × 150 = **600 mL of water**. Together, the five parts give 150 + 600 = **750 mL of drink** under our volume assumption.

You can check the relationship by dividing both ingredient amounts by 150. The amounts 150:600 reduce to 1:4, the original recipe.

This is why multiplying both sides of a ratio by the same positive number preserves it. You are resizing every part equally. Dividing both sides by the same positive number works too, which is useful when making less.

Adding the same amount to both ingredients does something different. Starting with 100 mL of syrup and 400 mL of water, adding 100 mL to each gives 200:500. That reduces to 2:5, not 1:4. You doubled the syrup but increased the water by only a quarter, so the new drink has a larger syrup share.

## Working backward from a full jug

Now suppose you need 1,000 mL of the original 1:4 drink. You know the whole amount, not the syrup amount. The five-part picture still works: divide the whole into five equal shares.

Each part is 1,000 ÷ 5 = **200 mL**. Use one part of syrup, or 200 mL, and four parts of water, or 800 mL.

Two checks catch different mistakes. First, 200 + 800 = 1,000, so you filled the requested amount. Second, 800 ÷ 200 = 4, so there are four measures of water per measure of syrup. A batch can have the right total and still have the wrong ratio; check both.

Before calculating, put all volumes in the same unit. One litre is 1,000 mL, so 0.5 litres of syrup and 2,000 mL of water are 500:2,000, which reduces to 1:4. Comparing the bare numbers 0.5 and 2,000 would compare different-sized units.

## Your turn: a different recipe for a smaller bottle

A new recipe uses syrup and water in a **2:3 ratio**. You want **750 mL** altogether. Using paper and a calculator if helpful, find how much of each ingredient you need. Explain how you know both the total and the relationship are correct before reading on. You do not need to prepare the drink.

Hint if needed: count all the parts first. Two syrup parts mean syrup occupies more than a single share.

### Check your reasoning

There are 2 + 3 = five parts. Each is 750 ÷ 5 = 150 mL. Syrup needs two parts, or **300 mL**; water needs three, or **450 mL**. The total is 750 mL, and dividing both amounts by 150 gives 2:3.

If you found 150 mL of syrup and 600 mL of water, you carried over the old 1:4 recipe. If you found 500 mL of syrup by taking two thirds of 750, you treated a comparison with water as a fraction of the whole. Syrup is two of the five total parts, so its share is 2/5, not 2/3.

For your next recipe, write down the ingredient order, make the units match, and picture equal parts. When you know one ingredient's amount, use its number of parts to find the size of one part. When you know the total, divide by the total number of parts instead. Then build each ingredient amount from those shares and check both the total and the ratio.

## Why these choices serve the goal

- The opening gives a familiar reason to care before introducing notation. Later sections follow questions raised by the recipe rather than generic “concept,” “analogy,” and “application” labels.
- The measuring-cup picture explains equal parts and its own boundary. Fractions and unit conversion appear because the practical task needs them, not to expand into a general mathematics course.
- The worked calculations expose intermediate steps and two distinct checks. The addition counterexample shows why a tempting alternative fails.
- The independent attempt changes the recipe as well as the quantity. Explained feedback diagnoses plausible mistakes, allowing self-study without inventing learner performance.
- The ending gives a reusable method within the stated goal. The chapter does not claim that the learner has mastered ratios or that this narrow example covers the entire subject.
