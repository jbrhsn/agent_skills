# Evidence-Preserving Refinement

Use this skill when the user wants a source draft, article brief, essay, or post refined through factual validation and real-world evidence without turning it into a different piece of writing.

## Core outcome

Improve reliability and clarity while preserving the author's original argument, personal perspective, intended audience, voice, tone, and level of ambition. Do not silently replace the author's thesis with a stronger or more fashionable thesis.

## Workflow

1. Read the complete source draft and any applicable repository instructions. Identify the author's central message, supporting ideas, personal experiences, tone, and intended deliverable.
2. Extract factual claims, statistics, forecasts, causal claims, and claims about current products, jobs, markets, laws, or public behavior. Separate these from opinions, advice, interpretation, and personal experience.
3. Research claims that are time-sensitive, disputed, numerical, or consequential. Browse when current evidence is required. Prefer primary sources, official reports, original research, and clearly described surveys. Record the source date, population, geography, methodology, and limitations when they affect interpretation.
4. Build an internal claim check before rewriting: supported, partially supported, unsupported, contradicted, or personal/editorial. Never invent a source, statistic, quote, experience, or causal explanation.
5. Preserve supported claims and personal material. For unsupported or overbroad claims, choose the smallest faithful correction: add evidence, qualify the wording, remove the number, recast it as opinion, or remove the claim. Do not add a new idea merely because research makes it available.
6. Remove internal contradictions, especially claims that both dismiss and require expertise, or claims that make incompatible forecasts. Keep the author's intended message by resolving the contradiction at the narrowest point.
7. Refine the prose in the author's existing style. Retain first-person language, examples, rhythm, directness, and practical advice unless they create a factual or logical problem. Do not make the draft sound like a research report.
8. Add numbered inline references such as `[1]` immediately after externally verifiable claims when the user requests references. End with a compact numbered reference list containing direct links. Keep personal observations and ordinary advice uncited unless they rely on an external claim.
9. Verify that every inline reference maps to one source, every important numerical claim has support, and no source is cited for a claim it does not actually establish. State evidence limitations in the draft or handoff when relevant.

## Non-negotiable boundaries

- Do not change the author's core message, voice, tone, style, or intended audience.
- Do not convert uncertainty into certainty. Avoid absolutes such as “always,” “never,” “everyone,” and “no longer” unless the evidence genuinely supports them.
- Do not treat a vendor survey, self-reported adoption statistic, or forecast as universal fact. Identify who was surveyed and what the result measures.
- Do not use research to erase the author's lived experience. Distinguish firsthand experience from generalization.
- Do not add URLs to a publishable post body when repository instructions prohibit them. Put sources in numbered references or a separate research-notes section.
- Follow applicable repository style rules, including the repository's zero-em-dash constraint when present.
- If the evidence cannot support a claim, say so and either soften or remove it. Do not fabricate a supporting citation.

## Deliverable

When editing is requested, update the requested source file and report what changed, which claims were validated or qualified, and any important evidence gaps. When the user asks only for critique, do not modify files.
